01 / Temperature & metal

Advanced NiTi
metallurgy

A file warms up inside the tooth.

NiTi is an alloy of nickel and titanium. Its atoms can change arrangement while the metal stays solid. These different arrangements, called phases, affect how easily the file bends.

Martensite is easier to bend. Austenite is generally stiffer and tends to spring back. Heat treatment changes the temperatures at which the metal moves between phases. Some files also pass through an intermediate R-phase.

Af means austenite finish temperature: the point at which the change to austenite finishes during heating. An Af above body temperature can help retain martensite or R-phase inside the metal. Some files lose cyclic-fatigue resistance as their austenite proportion increases at body temperature. In an apical curve, breakage risk also depends on the file design, curve tightness, and technique.

Why Af is only part of the explanation

Af above 37°C does not mean a file is entirely martensitic at 37°C. The full heating and cooling history, applied stress, and file design also matter. Austenitic NiTi can still flex through stress-induced transformation. Af is not a stand-alone safety rating.

Read the research: Arias et al., 2018 ↗ · Kasuga et al., 2023 ↗

FlexION in the apical 2 mm

A bend test at room temperature may not describe how the same file behaves clinically at 37°C — body temperature.

File manufacture must account for how the last 2 mm of a nickel-titanium file behaves deep inside a root canal, particularly through severe apical curvatures. As the file warms towards body temperature, its balance of crystallographic phases can change.

In some NiTi files, an increased proportion of austenite at body temperature is associated with greater stiffness and lower cyclic-fatigue resistance. Repeated bending in a tight curve can then lead to fatigue cracking and file separation. The risk also depends on file geometry, loading, and technique.

FlexION technology is designed with the aim of retaining martensite and R-phase at clinically relevant temperatures, to maintain flexibility when negotiating demanding apical anatomy.

02 / Bend & recovery

Controlled
flexibility

Less force to make the bend.

A flexible file needs less force to follow a curve. Its alloy, thickness, taper, and cross-section all affect that bending force. Heat treatment can reduce stiffness, but the finished file needs testing at a relevant temperature.

As a file rotates in a curve, the metal repeatedly stretches on one side and compresses on the other. Repeated loading can start a crack. This is cyclic fatigue.

Torsional overload is different: part of the file binds while the motor keeps turning. Greater flexibility or a longer fatigue life does not automatically mean greater resistance to twisting.

03 / The shape inside the tooth

Curved
canal anatomy

The tightness of the bend matters.

A root canal is a small passage inside the tooth. It can bend in more than one direction. A two-dimensional X-ray may hide a curve pointing toward or away from the viewer.

Two canals can turn through the same angle yet place different demands on a file. A tighter bend has a smaller radius of curvature, so the file bends more sharply over a shorter distance.

In Pruett and colleagues’ laboratory study, tighter curves and larger instruments shortened fatigue life. Files broke around the point of greatest bending. That point depends on the canal and is not always at the tip.

Shaping aims to preserve the canal’s original route while making space for cleaning. Flexibility helps, but anatomy, file selection, and technique still matter. Follow the file system’s instructions for use.
