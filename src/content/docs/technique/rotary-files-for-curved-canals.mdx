## Introduction

Managing curved root canals remains one of the most demanding aspects of endodontic therapy. The challenge is not simply reaching working length. The clinical objective is to reach working length without forcing the file away from the original canal anatomy.

<div class="clinical-guide-note">
  <span aria-hidden="true">i</span>
  <div>
    <strong>Navigation challenge</strong>
    <p>In curved canals, rotary files must balance flexibility, cutting efficiency, fatigue resistance, and apical guidance. If any part of that balance is lost, the file can begin to straighten the canal or engage the outer wall too aggressively.</p>
  </div>
</div>

## Clinical Problem

Curved canals increase the consequences of every mechanical decision. The clinician needs a sequence that reduces negotiation load before shaping and manages file behaviour as curvature intensifies.

<table class="clinical-guide-table">
  <thead>
    <tr>
      <th>Risk factor</th>
      <th>Clinical consequence</th>
    </tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr>
      <td>
        <strong>Straightening tendency</strong>
        <span>Instrument stiffness resisting curvature</span>
      </td>
      <td>Canal transportation and loss of original anatomy.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>
        <strong>Lateral wall stress</strong>
        <span>High cyclic fatigue demand in curvature</span>
      </td>
      <td>Greater file fatigue and more stress against the outer wall.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>
        <strong>Incomplete glide path</strong>
        <span>Poor initial scouting or patency control</span>
      </td>
      <td>Shaping files carry too much negotiation load.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>
        <strong>Aggressive apical engagement</strong>
        <span>Excessive tip pressure near the foramen</span>
      </td>
      <td>Higher risk of ledging, zipping, or loss of working-length control.</td>
    </tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
